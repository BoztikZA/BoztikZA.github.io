How to Remove Objects From Photos in Photoshop

Removing an object is really a reconstruction job: whatever was behind it has to be rebuilt convincingly. Here is how to choose the right tool and avoid the tells.

Removal is really reconstruction

Taking something out of a photo is the easy half. The hard half is rebuilding what was behind it, so that the viewer never suspects anything was there. A passer-by on a beach is simple: it is sand and sea. A person standing in front of a fence, a patterned wall or someone's arm is not, because the missing background has structure that has to be continued correctly.

This guide is for people who know the basics of Photoshop layers and want to remove distractions cleanly. If layers are new to you, read the layers and masks guide first.

Choose the right tool

Tool names and availability vary by Photoshop version.
SituationTool to tryWhy
Small spots, wires, blemishesSpot Healing BrushFast, blends automatically
Medium objects on a simple backgroundRemove Tool (newer versions) or Content-Aware FillFills from surroundings with little setup
Edges, lines, patternsClone StampYou control exactly which pixels are copied
Large areas, complex scenesCombination, or Generative Fill where availableNeeds rebuilding; always check the result

Limits to be aware of

The Remove tool and Generative Fill depend on your Photoshop version and plan, and they generate content that was not in your photo. Do not use them on photographs where the real content of the picture matters, such as a documentary or historical record, without saying so.

Step by step: a clean removal

  1. Work on a copy, on a new layer.

    Create a blank layer above your photo so your repairs sit separately and can be hidden or erased.

  2. Zoom in and look at what is behind the object.

    Decide what should be there: sky, grass, a wall, a continuation of a line. This tells you which tool and which source area to use.

  3. Make a selection around the object, slightly loose.

    Use the Lasso (L) or, for people and clear shapes, Select > Subject and refine. Leave a small margin so edges, hair and shadows are included.

  4. Fill from the surroundings.

    In Photoshop

    With a selection active, choose Edit > Content-Aware Fill (the name and workspace differ slightly between versions), check the sampling area preview, and set Output To to New Layer. In newer versions you can also paint over the object with the Remove Tool.

  5. Remove the shadow and any reflection.

    Shadows and reflections give a removal away. Select and remove them too, or clone nearby shadow tone over them.

  6. Fix lines and patterns by hand.

    In Photoshop

    Use the Clone Stamp (S) with a soft brush at low opacity. Alt+click a clean source, set Sample to Current & Below, and paint on your blank layer. Line up fence slats, paving or horizon lines before you stamp.

  7. Match grain and texture.

    Smooth patches stand out against a grainy photo. If the filled area looks too clean, add a little matching noise or clone real texture over it.

  8. Check at different zoom levels.

    Look at 100 per cent, then at fit-to-screen. Toggle your repair layer off and on to be sure the change is invisible, not just plausible.

What gives a removal away

  • Repeating patterns. The same patch of grass or cloud appears twice. Re-sample from different places.
  • Smudged texture. A fill that is softer than its surroundings. Add grain or use the Clone Stamp.
  • Broken lines. A horizon, kerb or pole that no longer lines up.
  • Leftover shadows and halos. A faint outline where the object was.
  • Missing context. A hand on a shoulder with no person attached. Check the edges of the picture.

Troubleshooting

  • The fill pulls in the object itself. Reduce the sampling area, or cover the unwanted source in the fill settings.
  • Colour or brightness mismatch. Add a small Curves adjustment on a mask over the patch.
  • Hair or fine edges look chopped. Paint the edge by hand with a small brush, rather than relying on automatic fill.
  • Result looks "made up". Use less automation, and copy real pixels from nearby.

Think before you remove

Removing people or objects changes what the photograph says. For family photos that is often fine, but for records, news or evidence it can mislead. Be honest about edited images when it matters.

Key takeaways

  • Work on a separate layer so you can undo.
  • Choose the tool by situation; use hand tools for lines and edges.
  • Remove shadows and reflections as well as the object.
  • Match grain and check at several zoom levels.
  • Be careful with generated fills; compare them to the real scene.

After a removal, check you have not pushed the photo too far with the editing mistakes guide.
