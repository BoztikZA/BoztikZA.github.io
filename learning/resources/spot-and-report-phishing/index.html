How to Spot a Phishing Email, Text or WhatsApp Message

Phishing messages imitate trusted people and organisations to steal information or money. Learn a safe pause-check-report routine.

8 min read · Published 2026-11-26 · Boztik Learning Editorial Team

What phishing looks like today

Phishing is a form of social engineering. An attacker sends a message that tries to make you reveal information, open a harmful attachment, approve a login or send money. The message may arrive by email, SMS, messaging app, social media or phone.

Some scams contain spelling mistakes or strange formatting, but many are carefully written and visually convincing. Good grammar, a familiar logo or your name in the greeting does not prove that a message is genuine.

Warning signs worth checking

  • Unexpected urgency: a threat of account closure, a payment deadline or a demand to act immediately.
  • Requests for secrets: passwords, one-time codes, PINs, banking details or identity documents.
  • Unexpected attachments: invoices, delivery notices or documents you were not expecting.
  • Link mismatch: the visible wording suggests one organisation but the actual destination is different, shortened or misspelled.
  • Unusual payment instructions: a changed bank account, gift-card request or pressure to keep a transaction secret.
  • Unexpected contact: a message that claims to be from a colleague, supplier or family member but asks you to bypass normal checks.

One warning sign is enough to pause. You do not need to prove a message is malicious before choosing a safer route.

Check through a separate trusted route

Do not use the phone number, reply address or link supplied in the suspicious message. Open the organisation's official app, type its known website address yourself or call a number from a statement or official contact page. For a workplace request, confirm through your normal internal channel.

For links, inspect the full domain carefully. A familiar brand name appearing somewhere inside a long address does not mean the brand owns that domain. If you are unsure, do not open it.

If you receive a suspicious message

  1. Do not reply, click, download, scan a QR code or approve a login request.
  2. Report it using your email or messaging service's reporting option.
  3. If it impersonates a bank, employer or service, contact that organisation using independently verified details.
  4. Delete or quarantine the message after reporting, following your organisation's process if it is a work device.

Never forward a suspicious attachment to colleagues as a warning. Share the reporting route or a safe screenshot with sensitive information removed.

If you already clicked or shared information

Act quickly, but do not panic. If you entered a password, change it from the genuine service using a trusted device. Change it anywhere else you reused it, enable multi-factor authentication and review active sessions. If you shared banking information or sent money, contact your bank immediately through its official number. If you downloaded a file, disconnect the affected device from networks if you suspect malware and contact a trusted IT professional or your organisation's security team.

Report the incident honestly. Early reporting can limit damage and help protect other people. Do not delete evidence that your bank, employer or support team may need.

Build a simple habit

Use a pause-check-report routine whenever a message creates pressure or asks you to take an unusual action. Keep software updated, use unique passwords stored in a reputable password manager and turn on multi-factor authentication. These measures do not make phishing impossible, but they reduce the chance that one mistake becomes an account takeover.

For workplace payment changes or requests for sensitive data, use a second-person approval process rather than relying on the message alone.
