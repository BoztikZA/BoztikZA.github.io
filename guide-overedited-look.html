Photo Editing Mistakes That Make Photos Look Artificial

Most over-edited photos are given away by a handful of specific tells. Learn to spot them, and you can fix them or avoid them in the first place.

Why edits look fake

You usually notice an over-edited photo before you can say why. Skin looks like plastic, edges glow, colours buzz. These impressions come from a small number of specific mistakes, and most of them come from pushing one control too far. Once you can name them, you can find them in your own work and fix them.

This guide is for anyone whose edits feel "off", and it works through the most common tells, what causes each, and how to fix it in Photoshop.

A quick check before you export

  1. Step back. View the photo at a small size. Anything that jumps out is probably too strong.
  2. Compare with the original. Toggle your edit layers off and on. Does the edited version still look like the same moment?
  3. Zoom to 100 per cent. Check edges, skin and shadows for the tells below.
  4. Walk away for ten minutes and look again. Eyes adjust to a strong edit as you work.

Plastic skin

What it looks like: smooth, texture-free faces with no pores or fine lines.

Why it happens: blur, heavy noise reduction or aggressive "skin smoothing" removes texture along with blemishes.

How to fix it: heal individual blemishes instead of smoothing the whole face. If you used a blur or smoothing layer, lower its opacity or mask it back so some texture shows through.

In Photoshop

Select the smoothing layer and lower Opacity in the Layers panel, or add a layer mask and paint with black at low opacity over areas that need texture back. See the layers and masks guide.

Halos and glowing edges

What it looks like: bright or dark outlines along edges, such as around a head against the sky.

Why it happens: too much sharpening or contrast boosting, often with high Radius or Amount values.

How to fix it: reduce the amount and radius of sharpening, and sharpen only the parts that need it with a mask. Sharpen last, after other edits.

Oversaturated, unnatural colour

What it looks like: skin that is orange or red, grass that glows, skies that look painted.

Why it happens: pushing Saturation, or Vibrance and clarity, until the colours clip.

How to fix it: pull back the slider, and check skin tones first. Use a Hue/Saturation adjustment layer, mask it, and compare against the original.

Crushed shadows and blown highlights

What it looks like: black areas with no detail, or white patches with no texture.

Why it happens: heavy contrast or an aggressive curve.

How to fix it: open the Curves or Levels adjustment layer and soften the curve. Aim for a full tonal range, with detail still visible in both ends.

Mismatched composites

What it looks like: an added or replaced element that does not belong: wrong light direction, wrong colour, edges too crisp or too soft.

Why it happens: the new element was not matched to the photo it was placed in.

How to fix it: match lighting direction and colour, give the edges a slight soften, and add a shadow where the element touches the scene. If it still looks wrong, it may need a different source.

Over-smoothing and lost grain

What it looks like: a waxy or "painted" look, with detail smeared in hair, fabric and foliage.

Why it happens: strong noise reduction, particularly AI denoising, removes grain and fine detail together.

How to fix it: reduce the strength, or blend the result back with the original at lower opacity. A little natural grain is healthier than none.

Detail that was invented

Some automatic tools add detail that was not in the photo. It can look sharp but wrong, such as a changed face or extra texture. Compare the result with the original at 100 per cent; the AI vs manual editing guide covers this in more depth.

Limits to be aware of

What looks natural is partly a matter of taste and style. Some strong looks are intentional, and a stylised edit is not a mistake. This guide is about edits that were meant to look natural but do not.

Key takeaways

  • Most over-edited looks come from pushing one slider too far.
  • Heal blemishes individually; do not smooth whole faces.
  • Sharpen last, lightly and with a mask.
  • Compare with the original and step away before exporting.
  • Blend strong effects back with opacity or a mask.
